## One pull request, many eyes

For a developer, a pull request is mostly code and green tests. For me, a
lot more hangs on it: the feature itself, UI and UX, the docs, the marketing
colors, code quality and maintainability, how deep and how good the tests
really are, whether the requirements are met or quietly drifting away – and
quite a bit more.

Showing all of that in a way you grasp quickly is a challenge. For a single
pull request, dashboards and comments do the job. But once I want to keep an
eye on dozens of pull requests across a whole product, it gets hard. What's
left are dashboards for single checks – never everything at once.

## The needle in the haystack

In the end I'm looking for the needle in the haystack. And it looks
different for every role:

| Role | The needle |
|---|---|
| Developer | the one red test among a hundred green ones |
| Project lead | the feature that has been stuck for days |
| Test | the change that is barely tested |
| Customers | the requirement that wasn't implemented |
| Marketing | the wrong color in the new screen |
| Legal | the new license in the dependencies |
| Controlling | the effort that is getting out of hand |

## Reading is slow

I believe reading words is one of the poorer ways to take in a situation.
When a hundred pull requests say "ok", "in progress", "backlog" and
"cancelled" in the status column, the one row that says "failure" is hard to
find. As a color or an icon, it jumps right out.

## Feel it, don't read it

That's where my motive comes from: problems should be something you sense
and feel intuitively. I don't want to have to read to understand a
situation.

So besides text, visual cues matter most – colors, shapes and structures.
With large amounts of data, depth helps too: a space in 3D instead of flat
text in a table. Sound could play an important role as well, but in
everyday office life it's hard to fit into the workflows.

## Patterns everyone knows

When it comes to showing things, I like to borrow ideas and concepts that
already exist, because people understand them intuitively. Almost anyone
can read a road map and make sense of its structures and colors: wide
roads, dense cities, empty land in between. Apply that kind of view to other
data, and you quickly get a feel for a topic.

With [Horus](/en/tools/horus/) I tried this playfully on real Sphinx-Needs
data. The needs stand in space as a glowing city: every group a tower, every
need a floor, the color shows the status. Schema violations glow red – you
see them from afar without reading a single line.

## What science fiction already knew

<Scene monster="danwos-grid" wide alt="The danwos monster stands amazed in a 3D grid, surrounded by floating, connected objects" />

A strong source of ideas for me are the computer interfaces in science
fiction movies and series. Be it the colorful 2D layout of the consoles in
Star Trek or the 3D networks in which the android brains in "Ghost in the
Shell" think.

Often the ideas are pretty far out – and they show up surprisingly early.
In the 1995 classic "Hackers", files rise up as 3D towers, and a virus runs
across the screen as a cookie monster.

Today AI delivers data in amounts that people have to review, check and
approve. Every idea has to be welcome there, no matter how much it breaks
with the current state of things.

## A pull request to turn around

A small experiment shows what this could look like: a pull request as a
roll. Every disc is a quality criterion – tests, docs, requirements, legal
and more. Every disc is split again into pie pieces, the tests for example
into unit, integration and system. The color shows how well each piece is
doing.

From the outside you see at a glance whether something glows red anywhere.
Only when you want to look closer do you pull the roll apart and read
what's behind it.

## Dozens of pull requests at once

One roll helps with one pull request. It gets interesting with dozens.
Here, 48 pull requests from four layers of a product stand in space – app,
service, library and platform –, connected wherever one depends on another.
Most of them are green. Where it turns red and orange, you see it right
away, even among all the others. One click brings the roll up close.

## How mature is a tool?

What works for a pull request works for a whole tool too. Instead of
hundreds of check results, one picture shows how mature it is: around the
logo one ring per maturity level, one slice per area. The thick block shows
what is reached, above it what is going wrong right now, and in gray what
has already been thought of.

![Maturity rings around the deciwa monster, invented example](../../../assets/blog/quality-maturity/rings-front.png)

Still an idea – how it works is in
[How Raw Is Your Software?](/en/blog/quality-maturity/).

## Working inside the data

<Scene monster="danwos-vr" left wide alt="The danwos monster lies on a curved lounger wearing a VR headset, keyboard on the left, mouse on the right, and works" />

Virtual reality headsets could carry this new world, and with them other
models for the desk and the workplace. Instead of tables on two screens,
the project surrounds you as a space – and the problem glows right where it
is.

## Tools for views and visualization

I've built some of this already. Horus is the city of needs. Sphinx-Preview
shows what's behind a link when you hover over it, without leaving the page.
Sphinx-Data-Viewer turns large JSON data into a tree that you unfold only as
far as you need.
